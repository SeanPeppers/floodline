Floodline

Does quantum amplitude estimation's proven query-complexity edge over Monte Carlo actually show up when you point it at a real flood-risk number? We measured it on 110 years of Key West tide data instead of assuming the answer.

11–94× fewer queries than plain Monte Carlo, depending on target precision
1-in-22-yr event: today's probability of a Key West major flood year
0× advantage over the best classical method, honestly reported
See the real numbers ↓

The question

In a given year, at today's sea level, what's the odds Key West hits major flood stage?

Data: NOAA CO-OPS station 8724580 (Key West, FL) — one of the longest tide-gauge records in the US, 1913–2025. Annual peak water levels, shifted to 2025 sea level using the measured trend (2.65 mm/yr), fitted to a GEV distribution.

Threshold: NOAA's published "major flood" stage, 0.64 m above the tidal datum used here. Five of the 110 detrended years already reached it: 1944, 1965 (Betsy), 2005 (Wilma), 2017 (Irma), 2022 (Ian).

Target probability a = 0.0458 — about a 1-in-22-year event. Both the quantum and classical estimators below are racing to pin down this exact same number. Whether the GEV model itself is right is a separate question this project doesn't answer.

Why this needs a fast estimator at all: a flood-risk number that takes negligible queries to estimate to high precision is cheap to wire into every downstream decision — insurance pricing, levee design, evacuation planning — without the query cost itself becoming the bottleneck. That's the proven claim being tested here.

The claim with a proof behind it

Amplitude estimation needs O(1/ε) queries. Plain Monte Carlo needs O(1/ε²).

A 10-logical-qubit circuit loads the binned flood distribution, flips an objective qubit when the bin reaches major-flood stage, and runs maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation (MLAE) at geometrically increasing Grover depths. Three estimators were run at exactly matched query budgets, 500 seeded trials each: quantum MLAE, plain Monte Carlo, and stratified sampling — the "same structural prior" classical control.

Stratified sampling needs roughly 8× fewer queries than quantum MLAE at every precision above — it also reaches α ≈ 1 in this one-dimensional problem. The quadratic speedup shown here is specifically over plain Monte Carlo, not over the best classical method.

The honest verdict

A real, measured speedup — over the wrong baseline to call it an advantage.

What this demonstrates

  • The QAE vs. plain-MC quadratic query separation is real and measured on real flood data, not asserted: α=1.02 vs. α=1.93.
  • 11× fewer queries than plain MC at 2% relative error; ~94× fewer at 0.2%.
  • Simulator-only, ideal-circuit result — stated plainly, not hidden.

What it doesn't demonstrate

  • No advantage over the best classical method here. Stratified sampling (or just evaluating the fitted CDF) also reaches α≈1 in one dimension, needing ~8× fewer queries than MLAE at every ε.
  • No hardware speedup today. On IBM Heron's noise model (FakeFez), the signal is washed to a coin flip by Grover depth m=2.
  • Query count ≠ wall-clock time. One quantum query costs far more than one classical draw.
  • The flood model itself has uncertainty larger than the precision being chased — a sharper estimator of a doesn't make the real-world flood probability better known.
Pitch it as "we measured the provable part and identified exactly what is missing," not as "quantum beats classical."
