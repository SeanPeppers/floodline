Floodline

Does quantum amplitude estimation's proven query advantage over Monte Carlo survive a real flood-risk problem? We built a tide + rainfall model from 110 years of Key West data and raced it against a fair classical control, 2-D Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo, instead of assuming the answer.

  • 13–131× fewer queries than plain Monte Carlo, depending on target precision
  • 1 in 9 years compound event: minor-flood tide and a 10-year rain in the same year (a = 0.109)
  • 3.7–4.6× more queries than the best classical control. QAE still loses this race, and we report it.

The question

In a given year, at today's sea level, what are the odds Key West sees a compound flood event?

Compound, not single-variable: the annual peak water level reaches NWS minor flood stage (0.335 m above MHHW) and that year's heaviest 24-hour rainfall is at least a 10-year event (7.46 in). Tide comes from NOAA CO-OPS station 8724580 (Key West, FL), 1913–2025, shifted to 2025 sea level (2.65 mm/yr trend) and GEV-fit. Rain comes from NOAA Atlas 14's published 24-hour return-period depths for the same point, re-fit to a Gumbel distribution. A discretized Gaussian copula links them, using the sign and significance of tide–precipitation dependence that Wahl et al. 2015 (Nature Climate Change) measured directly at this Key West gauge. The exact correlation magnitude they report is in a figure, not a table, so this build uses a representative moderate positive τ = 0.3 as an explicit, flagged assumption, not their measured number.

Target probability a = 0.1090, about a 1-in-9-year event. Every estimator below races to pin down this exact same number.

Why this needs a fast estimator at all: a flood-risk number that takes few queries to estimate to high precision is cheap to wire into every downstream decision (insurance pricing, levee design, evacuation planning) without the query cost itself becoming the bottleneck. That's the proven claim being tested here.

The claim with a proof behind it

Amplitude estimation needs O(1/ε) queries. Plain Monte Carlo needs O(1/ε²).

A 17-logical-qubit circuit loads the tide marginal, controlled-loads one of 4 regime-conditional rain distributions, and flips an objective qubit only when both thresholds hit: a discretized Rosenblatt/copula construction, with no closed-form 2-D CDF handed to either estimator. Maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation (MLAE) runs at geometrically increasing Grover depths. Three estimators, exactly matched query budgets, 500 seeded trials each: quantum MLAE, plain Monte Carlo, and 2-D scrambled Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo, the honest classical control for a joint 2-D distribution.

Sobol QMC needs roughly 3.7–4.6× fewer queries than quantum MLAE across this range: a smarter classical method still wins here. But that margin is narrower than the single-variable build's 8× gap over 1-D stratified sampling. QMC's own exponent (1.16) is visibly above 1, the expected signature of quasi-Monte Carlo's discrepancy bound degrading with dimension. QAE's disadvantage shrinks as the problem gains a dimension. That narrowing is the finding here, not a quantum win.

The honest verdict

A real, measured speedup over plain Monte Carlo, and still a loss against the best classical method.

What this demonstrates

  • The QAE vs. plain-MC quadratic query separation is real and measured on a real 2-D compound tide+rain problem, not asserted: α=1.06 vs. α=2.06.
  • 13× fewer queries than plain MC at the loosest matched error; about 131× fewer at the tightest.
  • Against a fair 2-D classical control (Sobol QMC, not a method that cheats with exact CDF knowledge), QAE's gap narrows from the single-variable build's 8× to 3.7–4.6×, the direction the "QAE matters in higher dimensions" argument predicts.
  • Simulator-only, ideal-circuit result, stated plainly.

What it doesn't demonstrate

  • No advantage over the best classical method here. Sobol QMC still needs 3.7–4.6× fewer queries than MLAE at every ε tested.
  • No hardware speedup today. On IBM Heron's noise model (FakeFez), the readout is swamped by noise before any Grover step even runs: ideal P=0.109 at m=0 reads back as 0.425.
  • Query count ≠ wall-clock time. One quantum query costs far more than one classical draw.
  • The flood model itself has uncertainty larger than the precision being chased. A sharper estimator of a doesn't make the real-world flood probability better known, and the τ=0.3 tide–rain correlation is an assumption, not Key West's measured value.
Pitch it as "we measured the provable part, against a fair classical control, on a real 2-D problem, and found the gap narrows with dimension," not as "quantum beats classical."

Outlook · toy model, not the Key West result

Where quantum overtakes classical: add flood drivers.

QAE's query cost doesn't grow with the number of random drivers; Sobol QMC's does. A separate toy study keeps the measured MLAE cost from the real model and asks how many Sobol QMC queries an event built from d correlated drivers needs to reach the same error. Above the line, QMC needs more queries than QAE. QAE pulls ahead at about d = 3.4, 2.7 and 2.4 drivers for ε = 1e-3, 3e-4 and 1e-4.

Read this as a toy model, with three caveats.

  • The d-driver events are linear (a threshold on a sum), which is classically reducible: a smarter classical method could collapse them to one dimension.
  • At ε = 1e-4, the QMC costs for d ≥ 7 are extrapolated from fitted scaling, not run.
  • Query counts ignore circuit size and noise. A QAE query on a d-driver circuit is bigger, and today's hardware can't run even the 2-driver one.

The verdict above stands for the real two-driver Key West model.
